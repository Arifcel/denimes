Hire slower than feels comfortable

By Full Name Month DD, YYYY 6 min read

Every founder we back eventually reaches the week when the plan says ten new people and the calendar says there is time to properly meet three. This note is about what to do in that week.

The pressure to fill seats rarely comes from one place. A board slide compares headcount with the plan. A large customer asks when support will be staffed around the clock. An engineer who has carried too much for too long hints that she is tired. Each voice is reasonable, and together they make a hurried hire feel like the responsible choice.

The cost that never reaches the spreadsheet

A mismatched early hire costs far more than a salary. For months that person shapes how decisions get made, which habits spread and who else wants to join. By the time the fit is plainly wrong, the team has quietly rearranged itself around the gap, and unwinding it takes longer than the search would have.

We have watched good companies lose a year this way. Not to a competitor or a bad market, but to three or four people who were brought in under pressure and then trusted with the wrong things.

What a deliberate process looks like

Slow does not mean vague. The founders who hire well tend to write down, before a single interview, the two or three problems the new person must solve in their first six months. They share that page with every candidate and ask which of those problems they would start with, and why.

They also spend real time together before an offer. A paid working day, a joint customer call or a review of an actual pull request says more than a fifth round of conversation ever could. Candidates notice the difference, and the strongest ones usually prefer it.

Covering the gap while you wait

Waiting is only possible if the work still gets done. Before opening a role, decide what happens to that work if the right person takes four months to find. Sometimes the answer is a contractor with a clear end date, sometimes a founder picks it up, and sometimes the honest answer is that it can wait too.

Whatever the choice, say it out loud to the team. People tolerate a stretch of extra load far better when they know why it exists and roughly when it will end.

A simple test before every offer

Ask whether you would be glad to have this person in the room during the hardest week of the next two years. If the answer comes quickly, move fast. If you notice yourself building a case, keep looking. The extra weeks will feel expensive now and cheap in hindsight.

We are always happy to act as a second pair of eyes on a hiring plan. Founders in our portfolio can send one over at any stage, however rough.
