Our thesis

Conviction that compounds

We back a handful of exceptional founders early, stay beside them for a decade or longer, and judge our work by the companies still thriving long after the headlines have moved on.

Most of the value in private markets accrues to a small number of companies that keep getting better for a very long time. They rarely look inevitable at the start. They tend to be run by people who care more about the quality of the product than the size of the round, and who treat every hard year as information rather than as a verdict.

Denimes exists to find those companies before the consensus does, and then to stay out of their way while being unmistakably present when it matters. We write fewer cheques than most firms our size. We spend more time on each one, before and after the investment, because the decision to partner with a founder is the only decision we make that we cannot easily reverse.

We publish this page so that founders can decide whether we are the right partner before we ever meet. Disagreement is welcome.

What follows is not a set of rules. It is a description of how we think, written down so that founders, co-investors and our own team can hold us to it. We revisit it every year, and we expect parts of it to change as the world does.

Five principles behind every decision

Each principle below has cost us a deal at some point. That is how we know they are real. Taken together, they describe the kind of partner we try to be and the kind of company we hope to find.

  1. 01

    Durability over momentum

    A fast-growing chart is easy to admire and hard to underwrite. We look past the growth rate to the forces holding it up: customers who would be genuinely inconvenienced if the product vanished, unit economics that improve with scale rather than erode, and a cost of serving each user that falls every year.

    A useful test we borrow from engineering: ask what would have to break for this business to stop working, then ask how likely that is over ten years.

    We are comfortable passing on companies that are merely popular. Fashion changes quickly, and a business built on attention alone tends to spend its future defending its present. The companies we want are the ones that would still make sense if nobody were writing about them.

  2. 02

    Few bets, held with conviction

    We construct a deliberately small portfolio. Every company receives serious attention from a senior partner, and every investment is large enough to matter to the fund. That discipline forces us to say no far more often than yes, and it makes each yes a considered one.

    Concentration is uncomfortable by design. It means we cannot hide a weak decision among dozens of others, and it means we must do the work to understand a market deeply before we commit. When we do commit, we reserve capital to support the company through later rounds rather than chasing the next new name.

  3. 03

    Partnership built on candour

    The founders we admire most want the unvarnished version. They want to hear what worries us about a plan before it is executed, not after. We try to earn that trust early by being specific in our feedback, quick with our answers and honest when we simply do not know.

    The best board meeting is one where nothing said in the room is news to anyone in it.

    Candour runs both ways. We ask founders to bring us bad news as soon as they have it, and we promise that it will be met with help rather than with a lecture. Board meetings should be the least surprising hour of the quarter, because the real conversations have already happened.

  4. 04

    Patience as a strategy

    Great companies compound slowly and then all at once. Our funds are structured with long lives so that we never have to push a founder toward an early sale to meet our own deadlines. We would rather own a meaningful stake for fifteen years than trade it for a quick multiple.

    We plan each investment around a ten-year horizon and are rarely unhappy when it runs longer.

    Patience is not passivity. It means staying engaged through the quiet stretches, when the product is being rebuilt or a market is taking longer to arrive than anyone hoped. Those are often the years that decide the outcome, and they are the years when a steady partner is worth the most.

  5. 05

    A global field of view

    Exceptional founders turn up in every time zone, yet long-term capital still clusters in a few cities. Several of the sturdiest businesses in our experience grew up far from the usual hubs, by teams who learned to do more with less and to sell across borders from their first year.

    We meet founders where they already are, and when they are ready to cross a border we introduce them to operators and co-investors we have worked with on several continents. A wider lens also makes us better investors at home: the patterns that repeat across markets are usually the ones worth trusting.

What this means in practice

For founders, these principles translate into a simple set of commitments. We will make decisions quickly and explain our reasoning either way. We will be available when things are difficult, not only when they are going well. And we will measure our contribution by whether you would choose us again.

We would rather be the investor a founder calls first than the one with the largest cheque.

Meet the people who help us keep these promises on the partners page.

For our own investors, they mean a portfolio that is focused, closely supported and built to be held. We do not expect every company to succeed. We do expect every decision to be one we can explain, years later, without reaching for luck as the reason.
